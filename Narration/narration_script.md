## 1. Hook and context

Two points are moving along the same curve. The gold point comes from the left. The blue point comes from the right. Both inputs are getting closer to two. Look at the horizontal guides. Can you predict the height the points are approaching?

Both guides head toward height four. At input two itself, the filled point is at height one. The same graph answers two different questions. What happens near two? And what is the output at two? We will use this distinction to calculate a limit and test continuity.

## 2. What does a limit say?

In our notation, x is the input, f of x is its output, a is the input we approach, and L is the output we approach. Read the expression as: the limit of f of x as x approaches a is L.

Here is what that means. Pick any positive distance from L. If we take x close enough to a, the outputs will stay within that distance from L. We use points of the function's domain other than a itself.

The minus sign above a means approach through smaller inputs. The plus sign means approach through larger inputs. When the function has inputs nearby on both sides, the two-sided limit exists as a finite number exactly when the two one-sided limits exist and agree.

## 3. Read the graph of f

This is function f throughout the scene. Start with the gold point. At input one point five the output is three point five. At one point nine the output is three point nine. The left-hand limit is four.

Now follow the blue point. At two point five the output is four point five. At two point one the output is four point one. The right-hand limit is four too. The approaching heights agree.

The open circle at two comma four excludes that point. The filled point at two comma one gives the assigned value. So f of two is one. The two-sided limit is four. This difference will matter when we test continuity.

## 4. Calculate the limit of f

Here is the full definition of f. For x different from two, use the quotient. At x equal to two, use the second branch, which assigns one. Putting two into the quotient alone gives zero divided by zero, which is undefined.

Factor the numerator as x minus two times x plus two. Whenever x is different from two, x minus two is nonzero, so cancel the common factor. This leaves x plus two at every nearby input on either side.

Take the limit of x plus two as x approaches two. Two plus two is four. The simplification explains why both dots follow the line. At two the second branch still assigns one. The open circle and the filled point record those two facts.

## 5. Check continuity

At any input a, continuity requires three things. The function value must be defined. The two-sided limit must exist. And the two must agree. For f at two, the first two checks pass. The last one fails, because one and four are different. Assigning four at two would repair this removable discontinuity.

Now switch to a different function, g of x equals x squared plus one. The filled point at two comma five gives g of two. On the curve, both moving points approach height five. Squaring and adding one preserve the limit, so the limit is two squared plus one, or five. All three checks pass.

Finally, consider a jump in a third function, h. Its left branch approaches four, and its right branch approaches two. The point at height two gives h of two, but the common limit is missing. Changing the assigned value cannot change either approach.

## 6. Summary and credits

A limit describes the output that nearby inputs approach. The assigned value at the target input can be different. Continuity means the assigned value and the two-sided limit both exist and agree.

On your next graph, read the left approach, the right approach, and the filled point. Keeping those three observations separate tells you exactly which continuity check passes or fails.
